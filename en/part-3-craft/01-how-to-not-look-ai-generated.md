# How to Not Look AI-Generated

*A designer's guide to avoiding generic AI aesthetics and preserving authorship.*

The goal is not to imitate arbitrary "human imperfections," or to conceal the use of a tool. The goal is to make work in which the decisions feel authored.

An AI aesthetic often feels statistically plausible, highly organized, superficially polished, and strangely indifferent to its subject. AI-looking work is work in which too many decisions appear to have been made by default.

## 1. Start with a point of view before starting with a layout

Before deciding how the piece looks, decide what it is trying to say.

* What is surprising?
* What is uncomfortable?
* What deserves disproportionate attention?
* What can be removed?
* What should the reader remember five minutes later?

Without a strong editorial position, the design tool has to invent one. Automated systems tend to fill that vacuum with familiar conventions.

> **Principle:** Do not ask the layout to create the idea. The idea should create the layout.

## 2. Stop using formulaic visual hierarchy

A common AI-looking pattern is hierarchy that appears to exist because hierarchy is expected:

* Two-color headlines
* One arbitrary phrase in an accent color
* A highlighted keyword in every title
* Eyebrow label \+ enormous headline \+ supporting paragraph
* Repeated "important" words with no meaningful difference in importance

The problem is unearned emphasis. If everything follows the same hierarchy regardless of subject matter, the hierarchy is decorative rather than communicative.

> **Principle:** Emphasize something because the meaning requires emphasis.

> **Test:** If you changed the highlighted phrase and the design still worked equally well, the emphasis was probably arbitrary.

## 3. Remove unnecessary category labels

AI-generated and template-driven posts frequently use small labels such as `MARKETING`, `FRAMEWORK`, `STRATEGY`, `CASE STUDY`, `INSIGHT`, `GUIDE`, `01 / INTRODUCTION`.

> **Principle:** A label must tell the reader something they could not already infer. If deleting it causes no loss of meaning or orientation, delete it.

## 4. Stop summarizing what you just said

Headline. Explanation. Three bullets. Then a box repeating the same three ideas as "key takeaways." Then a final sentence restating them again.

> **Principle:** Every section should advance the argument. Repetition should be intentional — used for rhetoric, memory, or rhythm — not because the composition appears incomplete without a concluding box.

## 5. Avoid putting every idea into a container

Boxes, cards, pills, and grids become a problem when they replace composition. If every thought receives a rounded rectangle, every piece of information receives equal visual status, and the page begins to resemble a component library rather than an authored communication.

> **Principle:** Containment should express a real relationship. Use a box because certain things belong together — not because empty space feels uncomfortable. Whitespace itself can perform the separation.

## 6. Treat typography as evidence of authorship

Do not accept generated typography merely because it is legible. Inspect font choice, line length, line breaks, kerning, tracking, leading, optical alignment, capitalization, punctuation, widows, orphans, and the relationship between type scale and meaning.

> **Principle:** Typography should contain decisions that could only have been made after reading the words. A title broken across three lines should break where the language wants to break — not where a rectangle happened to end.

## 7. Look for local logic, not merely global neatness

A convincing whole can conceal weak local decisions. Inspect relationships individually:

* Why is this aligned to that?
* Why is this element larger?
* Why is this gap 48 pixels rather than 16?
* Why does this component exist?
* Why does this information appear here?
* Why are these two things grouped?

> **Principle:** Every local relationship should survive questioning.

## 8. Replace placeholder logic with real content

Generic content produces generic design: *"Grow your business," "Unlock your potential," "Transform your workflow," "$24K Revenue," "+47% Growth," "Sarah Johnson, CEO."* These placeholders teach the design to accommodate an imaginary average rather than the actual communication problem.

Use the real headline. Use realistic name lengths. Use the actual dataset. Use ugly numbers. Use unusually long product names. Use the genuine edge cases.

> **Principle:** Design around reality early enough that reality can affect the design.

## 9. Prioritize readability over information density

Tiny typography often means the content was never edited.

> **Principle:** Edit the information before compressing the typography. If the content cannot be read comfortably in its real viewing environment, there is too much content. Do not solve editorial indecision with 9-point type.

## 10. Remove decorative evidence of "design"

Common examples include random icons, sparkles, abstract blobs, gratuitous gradients, meaningless diagrams, floating circles, fake interface controls, decorative arrows, tiny captions, fake metadata, and illustrations whose only purpose is to occupy an empty quadrant.

Empty space is not unfinished.

> **Principle:** Every element must justify the attention it consumes. If removing something makes the communication clearer without making the idea weaker, remove it.

## 11. Do not use charts as intellectual decoration

A chart should represent real data whose structure matters. Do not add a line chart merely to imply growth. Do not add a radar chart to give five adjectives the appearance of measurement. Do not turn a simple percentage into an elaborate dashboard.

> **Principle:** Use visualization when the relationship in the data is easier to perceive visually than verbally. A chart is an explanatory instrument, not a sophistication texture.

## 12. Avoid the statistical-average aesthetic

Broad prompts encourage dominant patterns and defaults: *"Modern fintech website," "Premium wellness brand," "Futuristic AI company," "Minimal creative agency."* Each phrase already contains a visual average. If the prompt is only a category, expect a category average.

> **Principle:** Define what makes this project an exception to its category. Do not merely specify what the design should resemble — specify what it should refuse to resemble.

## 13. Build references from outside the same visual feedback loop

If every reference comes from the same design feeds, the output becomes increasingly similar. Look beyond contemporary interface galleries and social-post templates — look at editorial design, film titles, industrial objects, architecture, signage, packaging, old technical manuals, vernacular graphics, maps, museum catalogues, subcultures, historical advertising, physical materials, and the visual language of the subject itself.

> **Principle:** Research the world around the topic, not merely other designs about the topic. This creates visual decisions that originate in subject matter rather than design trends.

## 14. Introduce productive friction

Some friction forces a decision. Useful friction can mean refusing the first generation, producing incompatible directions, writing the concept before prompting, rebuilding rather than polishing, trying a composition that initially feels harder, or asking what assumption the model made without permission.

> **Principle:** Ease is useful for execution. It is dangerous when it removes the moments in which taste is exercised.

## 15. Avoid excessive flatness and uniformity

The problem is when consistency becomes the only compositional idea. Introduce deliberate contrast: large against small, dense against empty, loud against quiet, rigid against organic, image against type, symmetry against interruption, repetition against exception.

> **Principle:** A system needs moments in which the system yields to the content.

## 16. Do not manufacture "human imperfection"

Artificially distressing everything simply replaces one formula with another. Do not add fake photocopy noise, arbitrary misregistration, random handwriting, forced asymmetry, fake paper texture, or intentional typos merely to appear human.

> **Principle:** Imperfection should come from a process, an idea, a material, or a deliberate visual argument. Random error is no more human than perfect alignment.

## 17. Use a design system as a constraint, not as the source of the idea

Give the model the actual system: real components, design tokens, project files, constraints, and context. A design system answers *"How does our organization express this?"* — it should not answer *"What should we say?"*

> **Principle:** Let the system preserve identity after the idea exists. Do not let the system substitute for the idea.

## 18. Use AI in narrow, reversible stages

Use AI for narrow tasks: generate alternatives, prototype an interaction, extend an image, remove an object, explore a composition, write temporary data, translate formats, produce code, test variations, or accelerate repetitive production.

> **Principle:** Give AI jobs, not authorship. The more consequential the decision is to the identity of the work, the more directly a designer should own it.

## 19. Make the copy impossible to swap

Generic copy can make a thoughtful design feel generated. Watch for language that could belong to almost any company or subject: *"Unlock the power of…", "Transform the way you…", "Where innovation meets…", "The future of…", "Smarter. Faster. Better."* The problem is that nobody appears to have needed this particular sentence.

> **Principle:** Specificity is a form of authorship. Use concrete nouns. Use actual observations. Use unexpected details. Allow the writer's point of view to create constraints for the designer.

## 20. Do not make every piece prove that it belongs to the same template

Identity is recognizability across variation. Template dependency is recognizability because nothing meaningfully changes: same headline treatment, same cards, same icon position, same rhythm, same information density, same closing slide, same composition with a different noun.

> **Principle:** Build a recognizable point of view rather than a recognizable template. A strong identity should survive different compositions.

## 21. Eliminate template artifacts

Watch for arbitrary horizontal lines, decorative dividers, tiny footer elements, generic progress indicators, and repeated structural ornaments. These often reveal that the creator started with a template rather than an idea.

> **Principle:** Avoid design elements whose only justification is "the template had one."

## 22. Design for the scrolling decision

The reader makes the first decision extremely quickly: *"Is this worth stopping for?"* A technically polished post can still fail because it feels predictable before the reader processes any information.

> **Principle:** Optimize in this order — visual intrigue, immediate comprehension, readability, substance. Polish comes afterward.

## 23. Treat accessibility as a design constraint

Generated interfaces routinely fail on contrast, focus states, form labels, and keyboard use. Accessibility is also a legal requirement in more places: since 28 June 2025, the European Accessibility Act requires many consumer digital products and services sold in the EU to be accessible, with an exemption for microenterprises that provide services ([Directive 2019/882](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/dir/2019/882/oj/eng)).

Use [WCAG 2.2](https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG22/) level AA as the baseline. The failures to check first:

* Text contrast of at least 4.5:1, or 3:1 for large text.
* A visible focus indicator, and every action reachable by keyboard.
* A programmatic label on every form input.
* Pointer targets at least 24 × 24 CSS pixels.
* Meaning not carried by color alone.
* Beyond AA, also honor `prefers-reduced-motion`.

Test with a keyboard-only pass, a screen reader pass (VoiceOver or NVDA), and an automated checker such as axe. Automated checkers find only part of the problems.

> **Principle:** Constraints that include every reader produce better design for all of them. Treat contrast, focus, and labels as part of the visual system, not a compliance pass at the end.

## The professional AI-assisted workflow

Change where AI sits in the process.

**Stage 1 — Human intent.** Write the argument, hierarchy, audience, context, and constraints before generation. Know what the work needs to do.

**Stage 2 — Divergent generation.** Use AI to produce alternatives, references, rough structures, assets, or prototypes. Generate enough variation that the first plausible solution does not become the final direction.

**Stage 3 — Human selection.** Choose according to the strength of the idea — not the polish of the render.

**Stage 4 — Reconstruction.** Move the chosen direction into the real production environment: actual typefaces, real components, real copy, real data, real brand rules, real dimensions. Replace generic model decisions with deliberate ones.

**Stage 5 — Craft pass.** Inspect typography, spacing, hierarchy, color, imagery, alignment, interactions, data fidelity, and edge cases individually. Do not judge only the gestalt — zoom in.

**Stage 6 — Authorship pass.** Ask what in the work could only have come from this project. If the answer is "almost nothing," the piece is still too generic.

## Three final tests

**The substitution test.** Could I replace the subject with another company, industry, or topic and keep essentially the same design? If yes, the composition is probably too generic.

**The deletion test.** Which elements can I remove without reducing meaning, hierarchy, or emotional effect? Remove them.

**The explanation test.** Can I explain why each major visual decision exists? Not "because it looked good." Not "because the template needed something there." Not "because the AI generated it that way." A defensible answer connects the decision to content, audience, identity, medium, or concept.

## The underlying rule

AI-looking design is usually not caused by AI itself. It is caused by unearned decisions: generic hierarchy, generic imagery, generic language, generic composition, generic polish, generic irregularity.

AI naturally produces orderly hierarchy, consistent components, balanced spacing, categorization, and decorative visual aids. Human-feeling work tends to involve stronger editorial decisions instead: what to remove, what to exaggerate, what to leave imperfect, and what deserves attention.

Increase the number of decisions that originate in the specific idea.

> Authorship means: the work could not have looked exactly this way if it had been about something else. That is the standard.
